Land Clearing Permits in Clark County, WA: When You Need Approval

Published May 10, 2026 · Updated November 6, 2026 · Brynion Excavation

Quick Answer: Most land clearing and grading projects in Clark County require a permit. The threshold is disturbance of more than 500 square feet or 50 cubic yards. Projects over one acre also need a state NPDES Construction Stormwater permit. Critical areas near wetlands, steep slopes, or floodplains trigger additional review regardless of disturbance area.

Why Land Clearing and Grading Permits Matters in Clark County, WA

Property owners across Clark County, WA face unique challenges when it comes to land clearing and grading permits. The wet Pacific Northwest climate, clay-heavy soils, and county-specific permit requirements all shape what a successful project looks like on the ground. Whether you’re planning a new home, an outbuilding, a driveway, or a commercial development, understanding the fundamentals before work begins prevents costly mistakes and delays.

Our site preparation team has worked extensively throughout Clark County, WA, and the patterns we see on project after project are consistent. The sections below break down what you need to know to make informed decisions about your land.

When Do You Need a Grading Permit in Clark County?

Clark County requires a grading permit for any land disturbance exceeding 500 square feet, or for any grading volume exceeding 50 cubic yards, on property that doesn’t have a current active building permit covering the same disturbance area. This captures most residential site preparation projects—land clearing for a new home, driveway installation, lot grading, retaining wall construction, and similar work all typically require a permit. Exemptions are narrow and don’t cover most substantial earth-moving projects.

How to Apply for a Grading Permit in Clark County

Applications are submitted to Clark County Building Services. The application requires a site plan showing existing and proposed topography, drainage patterns, erosion control measures, and the location of any critical areas (wetlands, steep slopes, floodplain). For projects over one acre, a state NPDES permit application must be submitted simultaneously. Permit processing typically takes 2–6 weeks depending on current workload and application completeness. See Clark County Building Services for current fee schedules and application forms.

Critical Areas and How They Affect Your Permit

Clark County’s Critical Areas Ordinance (CAO) establishes buffers around wetlands, streams, steep slopes, and other sensitive features where grading and construction are restricted or prohibited. The standard wetland buffer in Clark County is 50–200 feet depending on wetland category and adjacent land use. Steep slope hazard areas (slopes over 15%) require additional analysis. If your parcel includes or is adjacent to any critical area, your grading permit application must address how the project avoids or minimizes impacts.

Erosion Control Requirements During Construction

All Clark County grading permits require a Stormwater Pollution Prevention Plan (SWPPP) and installation of Best Management Practices (BMPs) to control erosion and sediment during construction. Required BMPs typically include perimeter silt fence, construction entrance rock, inlet protection on any nearby storm drains, and a stabilized staging area. For larger projects, additional measures such as sediment ponds, check dams, or hydroseeding may be required. BMPs must be installed before work begins and maintained throughout the project.

Inspections Required for Clark County Grading Projects

Clark County grading permits require at least two inspections: a pre-construction inspection to verify erosion controls are in place before work begins, and a final inspection to verify the project was completed per the approved plans. Some projects require mid-construction inspections for specific elements like subgrade preparation or fill compaction. Schedule inspections through the Clark County permit portal—inspections are typically available within 1–3 business days of request. Failing to schedule required inspections can result in hold-ups on subsequent permits.

Common Reasons Grading Permits Are Denied or Delayed

The most common reasons Clark County grading permit applications are returned or denied: incomplete site plan (missing proposed grades, drainage arrows, or erosion control locations), failure to address critical area impacts, inconsistency between the grading plan and any associated building permit, and missing SWPPP for projects over one acre. Common Questions About Land Clearing and Grading Permits in Clark County, WA

Homeowners and developers planning projects in Clark County, WA frequently ask similar questions. Here are the most important ones answered directly:

How long does the process take?

Timeline depends heavily on site conditions, project scope, and permit processing time. A straightforward residential project in Clark County, WA typically takes 3–10 business days of active site work, not including permit lead time. Complex projects with significant grading, drainage infrastructure, or utility work can run 2–4 weeks. Weather delays are common during the November–April wet season, so build buffer into your schedule if you’re planning to break ground during those months.

What should I do before the contractor arrives?

Mark your property corners if you have survey stakes. Identify any private utilities you’re aware of—septic tanks, propane lines, irrigation systems—so they can be probed or potholed before excavation. Clear personal property and vehicles from the work area. If you have specific areas you want preserved (mature trees, existing landscaping), flag them clearly. A pre-work site walk with your contractor is the best way to ensure alignment before equipment arrives.

What equipment will be on my property?

Typical land clearing and grading permits projects in Clark County, WA use a combination of equipment depending on scope: tracked excavators for digging and loading, bulldozers for bulk grading and clearing, dump trucks for material hauling, and plate compactors or drum rollers for fill compaction. On tight or access-constrained lots, smaller equipment like mini-excavators and skid steers replace larger machines. We assess access constraints during our pre-bid site visit and select equipment accordingly.

Will my property be left in a usable state?

Yes. Our standard project close-out includes site cleanup, removal of excess material and debris, rough grading to a condition suitable for the next construction phase, and installation of erosion control measures required by your permit. We do not leave active erosion hazards or material piles that could migrate to neighboring properties or stormwater systems. If final landscaping or topsoil replacement is part of your project scope, that’s included in the contract and completed before final walkthrough.
