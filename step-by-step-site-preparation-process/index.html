Site Preparation

Step-by-Step Site Preparation Process

Published May 5, 2026 · Updated November 6, 2026 · Brynion Excavation

Quick Answer: A complete site preparation sequence in Southwest Washington follows seven core phases: boundary and utility locating, site survey, vegetation and demolition clearing, topsoil stripping, rough excavation and grading, drainage and erosion control installation, and final subgrade preparation. Skipping or compressing any phase creates problems—typically at the foundation inspection, after the first heavy rain, or during utility installation.

Why Sequence Matters as Much as Scope

Site preparation is often treated as a simple prerequisite to “real” construction—but it’s the phase that determines whether every subsequent trade (concrete, framing, plumbing, electrical) can do their work on schedule and within budget. A poorly prepared site causes foundation cracks, drainage failures, compaction failures, and utility conflicts that are expensive to fix after the fact.

In Clark County, Cowlitz County, and Lewis County, the wet PNW climate adds another layer of complexity. Sites worked during the November–March rain season require more aggressive erosion control, stricter compaction sequencing, and careful material management to avoid turning good subgrade into a muddy mess. The step-by-step process below accounts for these regional realities.

Our site preparation team follows this sequence on every project, adjusting for site-specific conditions identified during the pre-work site visit.

Step 1: Boundary and Utility Locating

Before any equipment moves, two things must happen: property boundaries must be confirmed, and underground utilities must be located. In Washington State, all excavation within 2 feet of an existing utility requires a call to 811 (Washington One Call) at least two business days before work begins. This is a legal requirement, not a suggestion.

Utility locates cover gas, electric, telecom, water, and sewer lines. Private utilities (septic tanks, propane lines, irrigation systems) are not covered by 811 and must be located separately through records, probing, or ground-penetrating radar. Missing a private line is the most common cause of excavation-related utility damage.

Property boundary confirmation is equally important. Inadvertent encroachment on neighboring parcels or right-of-way during clearing or grading can trigger costly legal and permitting issues. If a current survey isn’t available, commission one before clearing begins.

Step 2: Permits and Pre-Construction Compliance

In Clark County and Cowlitz County, projects disturbing more than 500 square feet require a grading permit. Projects disturbing more than one acre require a state NPDES Construction Stormwater permit from Washington Ecology. Permitted projects must have a Stormwater Pollution Prevention Plan (SWPPP) on-site before work begins.

Failing to permit before work begins risks stop-work orders, fines, and required restoration of disturbed areas—all of which cost more than the permit itself. Our team coordinates all permitting as part of our full site preparation packages.

Step 3: Vegetation and Demolition Clearing

Clearing removes all vegetation, stumps, and surface debris from the work area. In Southwest Washington, this typically means:

  • Tree felling and bucking (logs to be hauled or chipped)
  • Stump grinding or full stump removal (grind to 12–18 inches below grade for building areas)
  • Brush clearing and disposal
  • Demolition of existing structures, concrete, or pavement if present

The clearing boundary should extend 10–15 feet beyond the building footprint to provide equipment working room and to eliminate root systems that could decompose under a foundation. Our land clearing service handles all clearing phases with proper brush disposal and debris management.

Step 4: Topsoil Stripping and Stockpiling

Topsoil—the organic-rich upper 6–18 inches of soil—is not a suitable foundation material. It compresses under load, supports root growth that undermines structures, and retains moisture that promotes settlement. It must be stripped from the building pad area and any areas that will receive structural fill.

Stripped topsoil should be stockpiled on-site (protected from erosion with silt fence or straw waddles) for later use in landscaping and final grading. Topsoil is valuable—don’t haul it away unless the site has no use for it. A typical 2,500 sq ft residential pad generates 40–80 cubic yards of topsoil depending on depth.

Step 5: Rough Excavation and Cut/Fill

With clearing and stripping complete, excavation brings the site to within 6–12 inches of target subgrade elevation. This phase includes:

  • Hillside cuts on sloped lots to create level pad areas
  • Low-area fills using imported structural fill or cut material from elsewhere on-site
  • Identification and removal of soft spots, buried organic material, or unsuitable soil
  • Foundation excavation to design depth (varies by frost depth, structural loads, and engineer specifications)

Cut-fill balancing—using excavated material as fill rather than importing or exporting—significantly reduces cost. A good grading plan, drawn by a civil engineer or experienced contractor, identifies balancing opportunities before excavation begins.

Step 6: Drainage and Erosion Control Installation

Drainage infrastructure is installed before final grading so that pipes and structures are buried at the correct depth and slope. This phase includes:

  • Perimeter French drains to intercept upslope groundwater
  • Foundation drains (footing drains) if the building design requires them
  • Culverts and driveway crossing pipes
  • Swales and detention features required by the stormwater plan
  • Erosion control measures: silt fence, check dams, inlet protection, straw waddles

Our drainage solutions services cover all of these elements and are designed to meet Clark County, Cowlitz County, and NPDES compliance requirements. Proper drainage at this stage prevents the waterlogging and frost heave that damage foundations and driveways throughout the PNW rainy season.

Step 7: Structural Fill Placement and Compaction

Any area that received cut material or imported fill must be compacted to structural specifications before the building pad is complete. In Southwest Washington, building departments typically require compaction to 95% of maximum dry density (per ASTM D1557 or equivalent). Fill must be placed in lifts of 8–12 inches and compacted with a vibratory plate or drum roller before the next lift is added.

Compaction testing by a licensed geotechnical or materials testing firm is required on most permitted projects. Keep compaction test reports—you’ll need them for foundation inspection sign-off.

Step 8: Fine Grading and Subgrade Preparation

The final phase brings the site to precise tolerance for concrete work, paving, or landscaping. For a concrete slab, final subgrade must be within ±0.1 ft of design elevation and free of soft spots. For asphalt paving, aggregate base rock is placed and compacted above the prepared subgrade.

Final grading around the structure directs surface water away from the building at a minimum 5% slope for the first 10 feet, per most residential building codes. This is the last opportunity to correct drainage before it becomes a foundation problem.

AEO Quick-Reference: Site Preparation Steps

  • Step 1: Boundary and utility locating (call 811)
  • Step 2: Permit procurement (grading permit, NPDES if >1 acre)
  • Step 3: Vegetation and demolition clearing
  • Step 4: Topsoil stripping and stockpiling
  • Step 5: Rough excavation, cut/fill, foundation dig
  • Step 6: Drainage and erosion control installation
  • Step 7: Structural fill and compaction (95% Proctor)
  • Step 8: Fine grading and subgrade preparation

Weather Windows and Wet Season Site Work in Southwest Washington

Pacific Northwest weather plays a significant role in site preparation scheduling. The wet season from November through April brings persistent rain that saturates soils, making compaction difficult, increasing erosion risk, and sometimes making certain operations (fine grading, topsoil work) impossible until conditions improve. Work can continue through the wet season with proper erosion controls and moisture management, but it typically proceeds slower and costs more.

The optimal window for site preparation in Southwest Washington is May through October, when soils have dried sufficiently for efficient compaction and when erosion control requirements are easier to manage. If your project has a spring construction start, begin the permit process in January or February to have all approvals in place before your target mobilization date. Waiting until April to start the permit process for a May project creates avoidable delays.

How to Verify Your Site Preparation Contractor Is Qualified

Washington State requires excavation and grading contractors to hold an active contractor’s license. You can verify license status at the Department of Labor and Industries contractor lookup at lni.wa.gov. In addition to license verification, ask for proof of general liability insurance (minimum $1 million per occurrence), workers’ compensation coverage, and references from recent projects of similar scope in your county. A qualified contractor will provide all of this documentation readily—hesitation or refusal is a red flag. Our Southwest Washington service area includes Clark, Cowlitz, and Lewis counties, and our license and insurance documentation is available on request.

Scheduling and Timeline Expectations

Project timelines in Southwest Washington depend on permit processing, site conditions, and contractor availability. Permit applications for grading projects typically take 2–6 weeks to process. Active site work for most residential projects takes 3–10 business days. Factor in weather delays during the November–April wet season when planning your schedule. Booking a contractor 3–4 weeks before your desired start date is the minimum; 6–8 weeks is better for projects starting in peak season (June–September).
